I design systems before I build them. Given a problem, I start with
architecture — how the pieces fit, where the boundaries sit, what breaks
under load — and only then move to implementation. That order matters
more to me than any single language or framework.

Day to day, that means full-stack work: frontend interfaces, backend
services, the integration layer connecting them, and increasingly, AI/LLM
components woven into the systems themselves rather than bolted on top.
I'm equally comfortable owning a feature end to end or focused on the
architecture of a single piece that needs to hold up under real
conditions.

The same instinct shows up outside code. CAD design and hardware builds
follow the same discipline — plan the structure, understand the
constraints, then build something that actually works, not just
something that looks finished. I think that cross-over is part of what
makes the engineering decisions hold up: designing a system in CAD and
designing a backend service aren't as different as they sound.

Right now I'm focused on deepening the parts of this that scale —
production-grade backend architecture, and AI integration that's
reliable enough to depend on, not just impressive in a demo.
